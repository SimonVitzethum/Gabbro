**Gabbro is licensed under the GNU Affero General Public License, version 3**
([LICENSE](LICENSE)).

Two additions come with it. The first is an **additional permission** under AGPL §7 (it
*removes* a restriction); the second is the **condition it is tied to**.

---

## Addition 1 — What you write in Gabbro is yours

> **A program written in Gabbro is NOT a derived work of Gabbro.**
>
> This covers the Gabbro source itself and everything the compiler makes of it: the generated
> C, the generated annotations, the binaries.
>
> You may release your program under any license you like — including non-free, proprietary,
> closed. **The AGPL does not reach through to your program.**

*Without this addition the situation would be unclear, and for a technical reason: the
compiler places **template code** into the generated C — proof scaffolding, loop shapes, range
checks. That code comes from Gabbro. Which is exactly why the permission is stated here
instead of being left to interpretation.*

This is an additional permission in the sense of AGPL §7. You may remove it from your copy —
**but there is no reason to**, and whoever removes it only harms their own users.

## Addition 2 — The condition: say where the guarantee comes from, when you claim one

**Addition 1 holds as long as the following notice is preserved *in the case that matters*:**

> **If you present the program, its generated C or a binary built from it as FORMALLY
> VERIFIED, PROVED, or SECURE — in any wording that borrows the credibility of a proof —
> then the artefact carries the notice:**
>
> ```
> Generated by Gabbro — https://github.com/SimonVitzethum/Gabbro
> ```

**And if you claim none of that, you owe nothing.** No notice, no mention, no attribution —
ship it, sell it, close it. That is the whole condition, and it is narrower on purpose.

**Why it is tied to the claim and not to the code.** A guarantee is not a property of a binary;
it is a property of a chain of arguments, and that chain is checkable only if the reader can
find it. Whoever says *"verified"* is using the word to move risk to someone else — the buyer,
the auditor, the operator who now trusts the thing. The notice is what makes that word
checkable: it says which checker was used, so the reader can look up what it does and does not
claim (`README.md` §6 states exactly that, and it is shorter than most marketing pages).

*Whoever claims nothing has nothing to make checkable, and is asked for nothing.*

**Concretely:**

* **Generated C** — the compiler writes the notice into the header of every file it generates.
  You may remove it. Removing it costs you nothing **unless** you make a verification or
  security claim about the result; then it is the one act that voids Addition 1.
* **Binaries** — a notice as a readable string, findable with `strings`, is enough.
* **What is NOT required, even when you do claim it:** no notice in a user interface, no
  mention in advertising, no approval, no report, no royalties. **The notice lives in the file,
  not in the product.**

## Why the two additions belong together

**The AGPL is meant for the compiler, not for what it compiles.** Whoever changes Gabbro
itself and offers it as a service publishes the changes — that is the point of the AGPL and it
stands untouched.

**Whoever works with Gabbro owes nothing but a line.** That is the trade:

| | |
|---|---|
| **you give** | one comment line in the generated files — and only if you call the result verified or secure |
| **you get** | your program is yours, with no reach-through |

## A note on the legal position, so expectations are right

**Addition 1 is legally unproblematic** — an additional permission under §7 is explicitly
provided for, and granting permissions is always the copyright holder's to do.

**Addition 2 is not a restriction on the AGPL as applied to Gabbro itself**, but the
**condition of a voluntarily granted permission**. It bites where Gabbro actually holds
copyright: on the **template code inside the artefact**. Where an artefact contains no Gabbro
code, this condition has no basis — and then the ordinary rule applies anyway, that a tool's
output is not a derived work.

*Since 2026-09-16 the condition is narrower still: it applies only where a verification or
security claim is made. That is a deliberate give-away of the easy case — attribution for its
own sake — in order to keep the case that matters: a proof claim whose origin cannot be looked
up is a claim nobody can check.*

*The same construction as the GCC Runtime Library Exception, and for the same reason.*

**This is not legal advice.** Anyone who needs these additions to hold in a dispute should have
them checked by someone who carries liability for the answer.
